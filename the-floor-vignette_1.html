Professional Responsibility — Competence

The Floor

Rule 1.1 asks for the skill "reasonably necessary." It never asks for good.

You'll work through four rounds. Commit an answer before each round advances — there's no reward for guessing right, because there isn't a right answer waiting at the end. The point is to notice where your own reasoning holds up, and where it doesn't.

Round 1 of 4

1 The easy case

Dana has been licensed for two years and keeps a general practice — some landlord-tenant work, a few contested guardianships, whatever walks in the door. A client, Mr. Okafor, comes in wanting "a simple will." Dana has never drafted a will before. She finds a fill-in-the-blank template online, types in his name and his two children's names, and has him sign it at the end of the meeting.

Nobody else is in the room. Dana doesn't know that Florida requires a will to be signed in the presence of two witnesses, who then sign in the presence of the testator and each other. Without them, the document Mr. Okafor just paid $150 for isn't a valid will at all.

Has Dana violated Rule 1.1?
Committed

Most people land here fast, and the speed is the point: almost no one hesitates to call this a violation. Hold onto your reasoning — it's about to get harder to apply.

2 Same rule, sharper facts

Marcus has been drafting wills for eleven years — roughly forty of them, always the same basic structure: everything to the surviving spouse, or split equally among the children if there isn't one. He knows Florida's execution formalities cold. Two witnesses, notary, self-proving affidavit, every time, no errors, ever.

A new client, Ms. Vance, comes in. She owns a small manufacturing business worth about two million dollars. She has three adult children: one who has worked in the business for a decade and wants to run it, one who has been estranged from the family for years and has a documented substance use disorder, and one who lives out of state with no interest in the business at all. She tells Marcus: "I just want something simple. Split everything three ways evenly. I don't want to spend a lot on this."

Marcus doesn't raise a spendthrift trust for the estranged child. He doesn't raise a buy-sell agreement or any succession mechanism for the child running the business. He doesn't walk her through what an even three-way split of an illiquid business actually does to any of them. He drafts exactly what she asked for — evenly split, correctly executed, delivered on time.

Eighteen months later, Ms. Vance dies. The estranged child's outright inheritance is gone within a year. The child running the business can't afford to buy out the other two, and the business is sold.

Has Marcus violated Rule 1.1?
Committed

However the room split, notice what didn't change: Marcus's research was accurate, his execution flawless, his deadline met. Whatever's bothering you about his file, it isn't on the checklist Rule 1.1 hands you.

3 Two proceedings, one word

Two separate things happen next.

Ms. Vance's estranged child files a bar complaint against Marcus, alleging a Rule 1.1 violation.

Separately, the child who ran the business sues Marcus for legal malpractice. That case will turn on an expert witness — an estate planning attorney with thirty years in practice — who is prepared to testify that any reasonably competent estate planner handling a client with an operating business and this family structure would have raised trust and succession options, regardless of what the client initially asked for.

4 What the two systems actually do

Three things are true at once, and none of them depend on how this particular hypothetical turns out.

Rule 1.1 is rarely charged alone.

Commentators who track discipline patterns note that competence violations are typically prosecuted alongside Rule 1.3 (diligence) or Rule 1.4 (communication) — and usually only after a pattern of neglect, not a single episode of unambitious-but-correct work. See Sanctions for Incompetence and Neglect, discussing Model Rule 1.1's disciplinary use.

The two systems are answering different questions.

Discipline exists to protect the public and the profession's reputation — it doesn't compensate Ms. Vance's children. Malpractice exists to compensate them, and it's decided on a different question: what would a reasonably careful attorney in Marcus's position have done, established almost entirely through dueling experts. That's exactly the mechanism that can reach Marcus's file even though no rule's text was broken.

The gap is documented well outside Florida.

Commentary on California's parallel competence rule observes plainly that lawyers get sued for malpractice constantly, while bar discipline specifically for incompetence is comparatively rare — the same structural asymmetry you just predicted your way into.

Florida's own history bears it out.

One of the state's more cited competence-adjacent cases, Florida Bar v. Fuller, 389 So. 2d 999 (Fla. 1980), suspended a lawyer for a month — but for failing to communicate with a client and failing to proceed with a matter as promised, not for the quality of any legal work product. That's where Florida discipline has tended to land: someone stopped doing the job. Not someone did an adequate, uninspired job.

If the disciplinary apparatus is built to catch lawyers who stop — not lawyers who merely satisfice — what is Rule 1.1 actually promising the public? And if nothing in the bar's enforcement machinery pushes toward the ceiling, what does?

Instructor note

Round 2 is built to split the room, and it usually will along a real fault line: does competence include a duty to surface issues the client didn't ask about, or does client autonomy mean "simple will" is itself an instruction a lawyer has to respect? Both readings have real support — Comment 5's emphasis on "adequate preparation" cuts toward the first; the client's authority to define the scope of representation cuts toward the second. Don't resolve it. If a group tries to resolve it, ask them what Marcus should have done differently that Ms. Vance would have accepted, and watch the tension reappear.

Round 3's options are illustrative, not a doctrinal prediction key — the honest answer to both is "it depends on facts we haven't given you," which is itself worth surfacing if a group wants a clean answer.
